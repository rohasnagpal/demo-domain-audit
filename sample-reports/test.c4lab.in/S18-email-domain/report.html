highS18-001

SPF record missing

Control S18-SPF-001 · Email authentication · Authority RFC7208

What this control checks

Checks that the domain publishes an SPF record (a TXT record beginning v=spf1) listing the servers allowed to send its mail.

What we found

No TXT record beginning "v=spf1" is published at test.c4lab.in. Receivers checking SPF for mail from test.c4lab.in get the result "none".

Why it matters

SPF is how a receiving server asks "is this server allowed to send mail for test.c4lab.in?". With no record, every server on the internet is equally plausible, so receivers cannot use SPF to reject or flag mail sent by an impersonator.

Without SPF, DMARC can pass only through DKIM. Any of your legitimate services that do not sign with DKIM will fail DMARC once you enforce it.

Since February 2024 Google and Yahoo require SPF or DKIM on all mail they accept, and both for bulk senders. Mail from test.c4lab.in is more likely to be sent to spam or rejected.

How an attacker would use it

An attacker sends mail with the envelope sender and From: address set to someone@test.c4lab.in from their own server. With no SPF (and no enforcing DMARC) the receiving server has no domain policy to apply, so the message is judged only by content filters.

Why high

High when the domain receives mail (it is clearly in active use and worth impersonating); medium when it publishes no MX records. It would be critical only if combined with a missing DMARC record on a domain known to send payment or account mail.

How to fix it

  1. List every service that sends mail as @test.c4lab.in: your mailbox provider (DreamHost Mail), the website and its contact forms, newsletters, CRM, helpdesk and invoicing systems.
  2. Collect the SPF include for each from its documentation.
  3. Publish one TXT record at the domain apex. In your DNS hosting provider: Open the control panel of the provider that hosts the domain's DNS (the nameservers listed in this report) and add or edit the record there. A starting point based on the providers detected:
    test.c4lab.in.  3600  IN  TXT  "v=spf1 include:netblocks.dreamhost.com ~all"
  4. Keep the record to ten or fewer DNS-querying terms (include, a, mx, exists, redirect).
  5. Send a test message to a Gmail address and check "Show original": SPF should show PASS.

Rollback

Delete the TXT record; receivers return to SPF "none" within the TTL.

How to verify the fix

Run
dig test.c4lab.in TXT +short | grep spf1
Expect
Exactly one line beginning "v=spf1" and ending with ~all or -all.

highS18-002

DMARC record missing or invalid

Control S18-DMARC-001 · Email authentication · Authority RFC7489

What this control checks

Checks that exactly one DMARC record (v=DMARC1) is published at _dmarc.<domain>. DMARC tells receivers what to do with mail whose visible From: domain is not authenticated.

What we found

No TXT record beginning "v=DMARC1" is published at _dmarc.test.c4lab.in.

Why it matters

SPF and DKIM authenticate technical identities (the envelope sender and the signing domain), not the From: address the recipient reads. DMARC connects the two: it passes only when SPF or DKIM authenticates a domain that aligns with the visible From: domain.

With no DMARC record, receivers have no instruction from you about forged mail and send you no reports, so you cannot see who is sending mail as test.c4lab.in.

Google and Yahoo require a DMARC record for bulk senders (more than 5,000 messages a day to their users), and many receivers treat a missing record as a negative reputation signal.

How an attacker would use it

An attacker sends mail with From: accounts@test.c4lab.in from their own server, using an envelope domain they control so SPF passes for their domain. The visible From: is yours, there is no DMARC policy to check alignment, and the message reaches the inbox.

Why high

High: exact-domain impersonation is unrestricted and invisible to you. It is not critical because content filtering still applies and no record actively authorises the attacker.

How to fix it

  1. Publish a monitoring policy now so reports start arriving. In your DNS hosting provider: Open the control panel of the provider that hosts the domain's DNS (the nameservers listed in this report) and add or edit the record there.
    _dmarc.test.c4lab.in.  3600  IN  TXT  "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@test.c4lab.in; fo=1"
  2. Create the mailbox dmarc-reports@test.c4lab.in (or use a DMARC reporting service) and review the aggregate reports for two to four weeks.
  3. Fix SPF and DKIM for every legitimate sender that the reports show failing.
  4. Move to enforcement in stages:
    v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; pct=25; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@test.c4lab.in
v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@test.c4lab.in
v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@test.c4lab.in

Rollback

Set p=none or delete the record; it takes effect within the TTL.

How to verify the fix

Run
dig _dmarc.test.c4lab.in TXT +short
Expect
One record beginning "v=DMARC1".

mediumS18-003

DNSSEC not deployed

Control S18-DNSSEC-001 · DNS integrity · Authority RFC4033

What this control checks

Checks that the zone is DNSSEC-signed (DNSKEY and RRSIG records) and anchored at the parent zone with a DS record, so resolvers can detect forged DNS answers.

What we found

test.c4lab.in is not DNSSEC-signed: the parent zone has no DS record for it and the zone serves no DNSKEY records.

Why it matters

Without DNSSEC, a resolver cannot tell a genuine answer for test.c4lab.in from a forged one. Cache-poisoning attacks (such as the Kaminsky attack class) or an attacker on the network path can return false A or MX records, sending visitors to a fake website and mail to an attacker's server.

DNSSEC is also the foundation for DANE, which lets mail servers pin your MX certificates in DNS.

How an attacker would use it

A poisoned resolver at an ISP or on public Wi-Fi returns the attacker's IP address for test.c4lab.in. Visitors reach a cloned site (HTTPS warnings are the remaining defence), and other mail servers deliver mail for test.c4lab.in to the attacker's MX.

Why medium

Medium: forged DNS requires a privileged position or a resolver weakness, and HTTPS still protects web users who heed warnings. Most domains remain unsigned, but DNSSEC removes a whole class of redirection attacks.

How to fix it

  1. Enable signing at the DNS host. your DNS hosting provider: Enable DNSSEC signing at the DNS hosting provider, then add the DS record it gives you at the registrar.
  2. Copy the DS record values (key tag, algorithm, digest type and digest) that the DNS host gives you.
  3. Add the DS record at the registrar. GoDaddy: In GoDaddy open Domain Portfolio → the domain → DNS → DNSSEC → Add DS record.
  4. Wait for the parent zone to publish the DS record (usually under an hour; up to 48 hours for some TLDs), then verify.
  5. Important: if you ever move DNS hosting, remove the DS record at the registrar first and wait for its TTL to expire before switching, or validating resolvers will treat the domain as broken.

Rollback

Remove the DS record at the registrar first, wait for the parent TTL, then turn off signing.

How to verify the fix

Run
dig test.c4lab.in DS +short && dig test.c4lab.in A +dnssec | grep -E 'flags|RRSIG'
Expect
A DS record, RRSIG records in the answer, and the "ad" flag from a validating resolver (for example dig @1.1.1.1).

lowS18-005

MTA-STS not enforced

Control S18-MTASTS-001 · Mail transport security · Authority RFC8461

What this control checks

Checks that the domain publishes an MTA-STS policy in enforce mode, so other mail servers must deliver to it over TLS with a valid certificate.

What we found

No MTA-STS record is published at _mta-sts.test.c4lab.in, so other mail servers have no instruction to require TLS when delivering to test.c4lab.in.

Why it matters

SMTP between mail servers uses opportunistic TLS. The sending server asks for STARTTLS, but if the request is stripped or the certificate is invalid it normally continues in plaintext rather than failing.

An attacker positioned on the network path (a compromised router, a malicious Wi-Fi network or a BGP/DNS hijack) can remove STARTTLS from the conversation and read or alter mail sent to test.c4lab.in. MTA-STS lets you publish "only deliver to these MX hosts, only over valid TLS", and compliant senders such as Gmail and Microsoft 365 then refuse to downgrade.

How an attacker would use it

STARTTLS-stripping: an on-path attacker removes the STARTTLS capability from mx1.dreamhost.com's response, and the sender delivers password-reset emails, invoices or contracts to test.c4lab.in in plaintext, readable and modifiable in transit.

Why low

Low: exploitation requires a privileged network position, and major providers already use TLS by default. MTA-STS closes the downgrade gap.

How to fix it

  1. Create the policy file. Start in testing mode, listing every MX host:
    version: STSv1
mode: testing
mx: mx1.dreamhost.com
max_age: 86400
  2. Serve it at https://mta-sts.test.c4lab.in/.well-known/mta-sts.txt with a valid certificate for mta-sts.test.c4lab.in. Any static web host works: add the subdomain at your web host, or use a service such as Cloudflare Workers, GitHub Pages or your email provider's hosted MTA-STS.
  3. Announce it in DNS. Change the id whenever the policy changes. In your DNS hosting provider: Open the control panel of the provider that hosts the domain's DNS (the nameservers listed in this report) and add or edit the record there.
    _mta-sts.test.c4lab.in.  3600  IN  TXT  "v=STSv1; id=20261027T113809"
  4. Publish TLS-RPT (see S18-TLSRPT-001) so you receive failure reports.
  5. After two to four weeks of clean TLS reports, change mode to enforce, raise max_age to 604800 (one week) or more, and update the id.

Rollback

Set mode: none in the policy file and update the id; remove the record after max_age has passed.

"v=STSv1; id=..." and a policy containing "mode: enforce" with every MX host listed.

lowS18-006

CAA record missing

Control S18-CAA-001 · Certificate issuance · Authority RFC8659

What this control checks

Checks for CAA records naming the certificate authorities allowed to issue certificates for the domain. Since 2017 every public CA must check CAA before issuing.

What we found

No CAA records are published for test.c4lab.in, so any of the many publicly trusted certificate authorities may issue certificates for it.

Why it matters

Certificate authorities validate that a requester controls a domain, but that validation can be fooled: through a hijacked web host or DNS record, a BGP hijack of the validation traffic, or a mistake at one CA. CAA shrinks the attack surface from every public CA to the one or two you actually use.

The certificate currently served on https://test.c4lab.in was issued by your current certificate authority, so a CAA record can name that CA without affecting renewals.

How an attacker would use it

An attacker who briefly controls a validation path obtains a valid certificate for test.c4lab.in from a CA you do not use, then impersonates the website or intercepts its traffic with no browser warning.

Why low

Low: exploitation requires another weakness to pass CA validation, and Certificate Transparency logs would record the rogue certificate. CAA is a cheap hardening step.

How to fix it

  1. Publish CAA records naming your CA (<your CA's CAA domain, for example letsencrypt.org>) and an incident contact. In your DNS hosting provider: Open the control panel of the provider that hosts the domain's DNS (the nameservers listed in this report) and add or edit the record there.
    test.c4lab.in.  3600  IN  CAA  0 issue "<your CA's CAA domain, for example letsencrypt.org>"
test.c4lab.in.  3600  IN  CAA  0 issuewild ";"
test.c4lab.in.  3600  IN  CAA  0 iodef "mailto:security@test.c4lab.in"
  2. Add an issue line for every other CA that issues for the domain or its subdomains (for example a CDN, mail provider or managed hosting platform). Check existing certificates at https://crt.sh/?q=test.c4lab.in before publishing.
  3. issuewild ";" forbids wildcard certificates; omit that line if you use wildcards.

Rollback

Delete the CAA records; issuance is unrestricted again immediately after the TTL.

How to verify the fix

Run
dig test.c4lab.in CAA +short
Expect
0 issue "<your CA's CAA domain, for example letsencrypt.org>" (plus any others you added).

Glossary

Terms used in this report

SPF
Sender Policy Framework. A TXT record listing the servers allowed to send mail for a domain. Receivers check it against the envelope sender (Return-Path), not the From: address people see.
DKIM
DomainKeys Identified Mail. The sending service signs each message with a private key; receivers fetch the public key from <selector>._domainkey.<domain> to verify the message was not altered and came from a key the domain owner published.
DMARC
Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance. A TXT record at _dmarc.<domain> telling receivers what to do when a message's visible From: domain is not authenticated by an aligned SPF or DKIM pass, and where to send reports.
Alignment
DMARC passes only when the domain authenticated by SPF or DKIM matches (aligns with) the From: domain the recipient sees.
MTA-STS
SMTP MTA Strict Transport Security. Lets a domain require that other mail servers deliver to it only over TLS with a valid certificate, defeating downgrade and interception attacks.
TLS-RPT
SMTP TLS Reporting. A TXT record asking other mail servers to send daily reports about TLS failures when delivering to the domain.
STARTTLS
The SMTP command that upgrades a plaintext mail connection on port 25 to an encrypted TLS connection.
CAA
Certification Authority Authorization. DNS records naming the certificate authorities allowed to issue certificates for the domain.
DNSSEC
DNS Security Extensions. Cryptographic signatures on DNS records, anchored by a DS record at the parent zone, so resolvers can detect forged answers.
DS record
Delegation Signer. Published at the registrar (in the parent zone, such as .org) to link the parent's chain of trust to the domain's DNSSEC keys.
EPP status
Registry status codes such as clientTransferProhibited that control whether a domain can be transferred, updated or deleted.
